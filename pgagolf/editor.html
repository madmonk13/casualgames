Designing a good hole: a short guide

A summary of what course architects write about, turned into steps for this editor. Lengths are in yards. Sizes in the panels are metres: 1 m is about 1.1 yd.

1. Start at the green and work back

  • Architects usually design the green first, then decide what kind of approach shot the hole should ask for. After that they place the landing area and the tee.
  • Keep greens' contours in step with the rest of the hole. A wild green goes best at the end of a short, simple approach, not a long one.
  • A tiny, heavily sloped green suits a short second shot. A long approach deserves a bigger, flatter target. As a rule of thumb, green half-sizes run about 10–16 m. Small is 10, average 13, big 16.

2. Give the player a choice, not one line

  • The best holes offer a safe route and a bold route. The bold line, hugging trouble, earns an easier next shot. The safe line costs a longer or worse-angled approach.
  • Old-school advice: put the hazard where the ideal shot lands just beside it. The player should aim as close to it as they dare.
  • Avoid holes where only one shot works. Leave room to recover from a miss.

3. Fairway and landing areas

  • Par 3: a tee and a green, no fairway landing. Par 4: one landing area (the drive). Par 5: two (the drive and the lay-up).
  • A landing zone can pinch narrower the farther you hit it. That makes the long hitter choose between distance and safety.
  • Rule of thumb for this game: the fairway Width knob at about 28–40 m in the driving area and 22–30 m near the green. Below about 20 m feels harsh.
  • Doglegs: a bunker or trees on the inside corner tempt a cut across. Playing around the corner is safe but longer.

4. Bunkers

  • Every bunker should have a job: guarding the ideal line, defining a corner, or protecting one side of the green. Placement matters far more than number. Holes with 9+ bunkers are often criticised as cluttered. Two to five well-placed ones is plenty.
  • Fairway bunkers work best in the drive zone, about 220–280 yd from the tee, on the side of the better angle into the green.
  • A bunker short of the green makes players take more club and risk going long. Bunkers at the side guard one half of the green and reward approaching from the other side.
  • A centre-line bunker in the fairway forces a left-or-right decision. Architects use these sparingly, once or twice a round.
  • Greenside sand is hardest on weaker players. Leave one side of most greens open so a run-up shot is possible.
  • Use Reshape edge to give bunkers fingers, lips and kidney shapes. Natural, irregular edges look better than perfect ovals.

5. Water

  • Water is the strongest hazard, so use it with restraint: a carry or a lake on one side, not every hole.
  • Show its edges. Players should see where trouble starts.
  • Keep forced carries short for casual players: well under about 130 yd for an average golfer. Water across the whole approach is a "heroic" hole, so give it a bail-out side.
  • Water looks best when it looks native: low ground, a creek along a valley, a pond at the foot of a slope.

6. Ground: hills, mounds and slope

  • Contour is an underrated hazard. Humps, hollows and run-offs make lies and bounces interesting without adding more sand or water.
  • Use Hill / mound to tilt a landing area, so the ball feeds toward a bunker or away from it. Or add a hollow short of a green that swallows a weak approach.
  • Subtle green slopes matter more than you'd think: 1–2% is plenty, and over about 3% gets severe. Most greens tilt down toward the front, which helps hold approach shots.
  • Uphill holes play longer and downhill shorter, so weigh the Rise setting when picking par.

7. Par and length

Published ranges vary a little by source and year. Par really depends on effective length, so a steep uphill hole can earn an extra stroke. Short par 4s around 280–340 yd make great risk-reward holes. So do reachable par 5s around 480–520 yd.

8. Across a course

  • Mix lengths, directions and bends. Don't stack several long par 4s in a row.
  • Vary the hazards hole to hole: sand on one, water on the next, ground contour on another.
  • Use trees and deep rough with restraint. Wall-to-wall trouble isn't fun.

Quick checklist

  • Can you name the safe line and the bold line?
  • Does each bunker and pond have a job?
  • Is there a way to recover from a miss?
  • Is the green's size right for the length of the shot into it?
  • Play-test it: does the obvious shot feel slightly too risky?
